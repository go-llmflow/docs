Security

Security

On 2026-10-26 an independent, adversarial review of gateway v0.8.0 (commit b23064a) was made by an agent that did not write the code, together with chaos tests on the parts that keep state. Its fixes are released in v0.9.0. This page summarises it; the gateway’s README, section “Security audit 2026-10-26”, at the v0.9.0 tag is the source, and where the two differ the README wins.

Method

  • Read, then attack. The threat model and the code were read first. vLLM’s own sources were opened wherever the gateway relies on how vLLM parses a request (vllm-project/vllm main at 256d8b4: the scoring, embedding and Anthropic protocol files).
  • Scope. Every package of the module, and the modules it pulls for authentication and storage: go-authn/oidc v0.4.2, go-authn/rp v0.1.2, go-authn/scim v0.1.0 and go-volumes/s3 v0.2.0. The fixes of earlier reviews were rechecked and hold, with their tests.
  • A route by role matrix. TestAuthzMatrix calls every admin route of the router (a route without a row fails the test) as 12 principals on one organisation’s objects: the master key, proxy_admin, proxy_viewer, the org_admin of the organisation and of another one (the cross-organisation attacker), team_admin, member, a team viewer and an organisation viewer, a user without roles, an inference key, and a JWT-shaped bearer. Listings are checked for leaks to outsiders, and the matrix was checked against planted mistakes.
  • Fuzzing. The 29 existing targets ran for 10 minutes each on v0.8.0, three at a time with two workers each: none failed, and no new crasher was found. A new target, FuzzScopeEveryRoute, ran for 60 s with no failure; it caught a planted regression on its first seed and is part of the CI fuzz smoke.
  • govulncheck (vulnerability database of 2026-10-21) on the module and on the four modules above.

Each fix has a test that fails without it. A3 is the only one that crosses organisations; the matrix found no other cross-organisation hole on the 39 admin routes.

The pii defect (A9)

Its own README, at v0.2.0, says what was measured: 1 MiB of 1 1 1 ... took 11 min 56 s with v0.1.0 (measured 2026-10-26, on a loaded machine). Since v0.2.0 card candidates are bounded at 19 digits, patterns of bounded length are searched in 512-byte windows, and TestLongRunsStayLinear checks the growth from 128 KiB to 2 MiB on twelve adversarial inputs (linear is x16, and the test fails above x64). The gateway’s README gives the same defect as 5 to 20 minutes; the two figures were measured separately. Upgrade pii to v0.2.0 or later.

The hide_secrets guardrail uses gitsafe’s redact. Its README at v0.17.1 says that since that version it finds the issuers’ prefixes with strings.Index instead of stepping a regular-expression automaton through the whole text: identical results (compared on 20,000 generated texts and by a fuzz target), and about 30 times faster on the gateway’s 2 KB benchmark prompt, 4 us instead of 130 us on an Apple M4 Max on 2026-10-26, measured on a loaded machine, so only an order of magnitude.

Verified without a finding

No cross-organisation IDOR on the 39 admin routes; CSRF protection (synchronizer token, Origin, Sec-Fetch-Site) on every state change of the UI and of cookie-authenticated API calls; a fresh session at login, __Host- cookies, no open redirect; every outbound URL comes from the configuration and no client redirect is followed; keys, tokens and the master key compared in constant time over hashes and never logged; a fallback keeps the original organisation’s cache salt, uuids and priority; scheduled-rotation targets stay under their team’s Vault prefix; export keys validated against traversal; request bodies read only after authentication and bounded.

Against a real vLLM, the end-to-end run of bench v0.2.0 (2026-10-26) confirmed that a client’s priority, vllm_xargs, extra_body and cache_salt change neither the scheduling order nor the cache isolation, and that prefix-cache isolation per organisation holds: vLLM’s own counters showed 768 of 814 prefix tokens hit within one organisation and 0 of 814 from another.

Chaos results

PostgreSQL ran behind a fault-injecting proxy (the README names a real PostgreSQL) that could refuse connections or blackhole them.

PostgreSQL partitioned503 within 5 s; fail-closed on time; recovery when the partition heals.

Accounting during an outageWith v0.9.0, every record of the requests served during the outage was lost (21 of 21 in the test, 2026-10-26). With v0.10.0’s durable spool, 21 of 21 are stored once, with 21 distinct ids.

The accounting loss is fixed in v0.10.0. Every batch of usage records is appended to a spool file and fsync’d before it is inserted into PostgreSQL, and a record is stored at most once (its id is the request id). From the README of v0.10.0, measured on 2026-10-26: TestChaosAccountingOutage stores 21 of 21 records after a PostgreSQL outage, where v0.9.0 lost all 21; with the real binary, 20 records were spooled while PostgreSQL was unreachable, the gateway was killed with SIGKILL and restarted, and 21 of 21 records were stored, with 21 distinct ids and no double billing. This is not a promise of zero loss: a record still in the in-memory queue when the process dies (about the last second of traffic) is lost, and past spool_max_bytes (256 MiB per process by default) a batch the store refuses is lost too, counted in llmflow_accounting_failed_total and alerted as accounting_records_lost. store.spool_dir is mandatory with the postgres store since v0.10.0; see Upgrading to v0.10.0.

Dependencies

govulncheck (database of 2026-10-21) finds nothing in the gateway built with Go 1.27.3. go-authn/oidc v0.4.2, go-authn/rp v0.1.2 and go-volumes/s3 v0.2.0 declare go 1.27.1: built with that toolchain, nine standard-library vulnerabilities are reachable from them (net/http, crypto/tls, crypto/x509; fixed in Go 1.27.2 and 1.27.3). The gateway’s binary is not affected because it is built with 1.27.3, but those modules should raise their go line. go-authn/scim v0.1.0 is clean. The gateway v0.10.0 takes the new releases at the go 1.27.3 floor: oidc v0.4.3, rp v0.2.0, scim v0.1.1, s3 v0.2.1, pii v0.2.1.

Residual risks

  1. Usage served during a store outage is not recorded fixed in v0.10.0 (see above). What remains is the last second of in-memory queue on a crash, and a full spool.
  2. SCIM adopts an existing gateway user with the same userName, and re-enables it if a proxy admin had disabled it by hand. The identity provider is already trusted for usernames through SSO.
  3. Roles that SSO maps from ID-token claims are fixed at login, for the session’s lifetime. Stored roles and the disabled flag are read on every request.
  4. A member may point their own key’s scheduled rotation at any field under the team’s Vault prefix, overwriting a secret delivered there for another key of the team: a denial of service inside the team, with no read access.
  5. Secret files are read whatever their permissions (Kubernetes mounts secrets 0644 by default). Keep them in a directory only the gateway’s user can read.
  6. A tool server’s error message (at most 4 KiB) is returned to the MCP client: tool servers are configured, so they are trusted.

The gateway’s limitations list the open defects, re-checked in this audit.

Security fixes in v0.18.1

Not part of the 2026-10-26 audit above: two defects of v0.18.0 found by a post-merge review and fixed in v0.18.1 (gateway#100). Upgrade if you use workload profiles.

  • #98, a hard-budget hole. A rolling window kept only twice its current length of spend, so widening session_window (for example 1 h to 5 h) forgot spend that was inside the new window and a session budget could be exceeded. A rolling budget now keeps its buckets for the longest window that can apply (MaxRollingWindow, 7 days: at most 2016 buckets of 5 minutes per budget), on the memory and the PostgreSQL store. Spend lost by a v0.18.0 prune before the upgrade is not recoverable from the buckets; it is still in the usage records.
  • #99, an authorization bypass. An organisation admin could grant their organisation’s viewer role to any existing user, which made that person theirs to assign a profile, read and delete. Who a person belongs to is now decided by their team bindings (which SSO and SCIM also create). An organisation-level binding (viewer, org_admin) is authority, not membership: it gives no access to the person, whoever granted it. A person with only such bindings, or none, or a proxy role, belongs to the proxy admins. The same rule applies to the user budgets API. Behaviour change: an organisation admin no longer assigns the profile of another organisation admin who is in no team; ask a proxy admin.

Security fix in v0.18.2: who may place a person in an organisation (#104)

v0.18.1 still let an organisation admin pull a user who had no binding, or who belonged to another organisation, into their organisation by granting them a team role, and then act on them. Fixed in v0.18.2 (gateway#106). Every role binding now records its provenance: scim, sso_claim, proxy_admin or org_admin (empty for unknown or legacy bindings), shown as provenance in GET /admin/users/{name}/roles.

  • The first grant to a binding-less user, or to a user of another organisation, needs a proxy admin. An organisation or team admin may grant a role only to a user already provisioned into that organisation: one holding a team binding of that organisation created by SCIM, an SSO claim or a proxy admin. (A user created by an org admin needs a proxy admin’s first grant.)
  • A team binding that an org admin granted does not make the person theirs: it is ignored by the profile and user-budget access rules. SCIM re-creates a binding it also wants under its own provenance.
  • Migration. The column is added automatically (provenance text NOT NULL DEFAULT ''). Bindings stored before v0.18.2 have an unknown provenance, which is treated as legitimate: nothing says they were not, so no existing access changes at the upgrade. Only grants made after it are classified. Whether an old binding was in fact illegitimate is therefore not something the upgrade can tell you: review them.
